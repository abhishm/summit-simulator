

## Stated Positions on Core AI Safety Issues

Chinese experts advocate for a hybrid approach termed agile governance, balancing strict laws with dynamic industry standards [cite: 10]. While China was an early adopter of binding regulations for algorithmic recommendations, state-affiliated experts currently emphasize the necessity of non-regulatory technical standards to manage frontier risks without stifling competitiveness [cite: 7, 11]. Institutions like the National Technical Committee 260 (TC260) rapidly translate broad safety frameworks into detailed standards [cite: 2, 7, 12]. Concurrently, experts support voluntary industry commitments—such as the AI Industry Alliance's (AIIA) safety pledges—which mirror global norms established at international summits [cite: 13, 14]. To enforce these standards, experts are heavily invested in institutionalizing pre-deployment evaluations. Entities like CAICT and BAAI have developed comprehensive safety benchmarks, such as the ForesightSafety Bench, to evaluate models across dozens of risk categories prior to release [cite: 4, 15]. Liability is increasingly viewed through the lens of lifecycle management, with experts arguing that developers must submit safety cases to regulators and undergo independent third-party audits before deploying high-risk models [cite: 16]. 

Regarding the distribution of technology, experts position open-source AI as a shared asset for all humanity that democratizes technological access, particularly for developing nations [cite: 8]. Domestically, open-weight strategies are championed as a mechanism to build global developer ecosystems and bypass compute constraints imposed by foreign sanctions [cite: 17]. However, recognizing the severe risks of malicious downstream use, think tanks advocate for shifting safety responsibilities upstream. They require developers to implement rigorous safety pre-training, data curation, and risk thresholds before weights are published [cite: 18]. 

The position on export and compute controls reflects a complex evolution. While historically critical of foreign export controls on semiconductors, Chinese policy experts actively architected their own export control regime for frontier AI technologies. By late 2026, experts supported draft restrictions on the export of models exceeding two trillion parameters or $10^{26}$ training FLOPs [cite: 19, 20]. This is framed not as protectionism, but as a necessary safety mechanism to ensure frontier AI remains safe, controllable, and immune to malicious global actors [cite: 14, 21]. 

Finally, a core tenet of the Chinese expert position is the equitable distribution of AI benefits and the mitigation of labor impacts. Prominently featured in the Global AI Governance Initiative (GAIGI), this position frames Western AI dominance as a driver of inequality [cite: 8, 22]. Experts explicitly court the Global South by promoting capacity building, localized talent development, and accessible open-source models [cite: 11, 22]. They stress the need for systemic monitoring of AI's social impacts, including labor displacement, structural unemployment, and ecological footprints, ensuring that AI development aligns with broader societal and low-carbon goals [cite: 5, 8].

## Strategic Red Lines and Calculated Tradeoffs

State-affiliated experts navigate global governance by adhering to strict boundaries while strategically conceding ground on others. These parameters are defined by the imperative to protect state sovereignty while ensuring integration into the global technological ecosystem.

| Governance Domain | Non-Negotiable Red Lines | Calculated Tradeoffs |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Sovereignty & Intervention** | Unequivocally reject any global governance framework that asserts centralized, extraterritorial control over domestic AI development, data ecosystems, or ideological safety standards [cite: 11, 23, 24]. | Willing to adopt Western-aligned technical safety taxonomies and participate in international safety networks (like the network of AISIs via CNAISDA) to secure a seat at the global governance table [cite: 25, 26, 27]. |
| **Scientific Risk Thresholds** | Insist on strict prohibitions against the autonomous replication or self-improvement of AI, a total ban on AI delegation in nuclear command and control, and limits against deceptive alignment [cite: 4, 28, 29]. | Willing to compromise on absolute open-weight freedom by supporting domestic export controls on the highest-tier frontier models to mitigate global proliferation risks [cite: 14, 20, 21]. |

## Stakeholder Dynamics and Multistakeholder Engagement

Chinese state-affiliated experts consistently align with developing nations and the Global South. By offering accessible technology, infrastructure, and a governance model that respects state sovereignty over information control, they position themselves as the technological partner of choice for the global majority, filling a gap left by Western frameworks [cite: 11, 22, 30]. Conversely, severe friction exists with U.S. and Western policymakers over the deployment of export controls, the definition of algorithmic safety (which in China includes ideological conformity), and attempts to restrict Chinese participation in top-tier safety summits [cite: 11, 25, 31]. Internal friction also emerges between Chinese safety scientists advocating for strict legal enforcement of catastrophic risk thresholds and industrial policymakers prioritizing rapid commercialization [cite: 16, 32].

The behavioral posture of Chinese experts bifurcates distinctly depending on the nature of the multistakeholder forum, demonstrating a highly adaptable diplomatic strategy.

| Forum Type | Engagement Posture | Typical Messaging and Focus |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Formal Intergovernmental Arenas** (e.g., UN General Assembly, Summit Plenaries) | Highly rhetorical, politically disciplined, and state-aligned [cite: 23, 33]. | Emphasizes multilateralism, national sovereignty, the Global AI Governance Initiative, and the rights of developing nations. Resists exclusionary Western governance blocs [cite: 8, 9, 34]. |
| **Academic & Working Sessions** (e.g., Track II Dialogues, Bletchley Park Technical Groups) | Pragmatic, technical, and scientifically collaborative [cite: 14, 29]. | Focuses heavily on evaluation methodologies, incident taxonomies, and cybersecurity. Readily signs stringent consensus statements on behavioral red lines (e.g., International Dialogues on AI Safety) alongside Western counterparts [cite: 16, 28, 31]. |

Chinese state-affiliated AI governance experts operate at the nexus of scientific reality and geopolitical strategy. They project a governance model that aggressively pursues technological leadership and open-source proliferation while constructing rigorous, state-controlled boundaries around frontier capabilities, demanding that the international community engage with them on equal terms.
