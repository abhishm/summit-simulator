

## Governance Thresholds

### Red Lines (Non-Negotiable Conditions)
The structural non-negotiable conditions for these stakeholders center on preventing human rights abuses and safeguarding sovereign autonomy. They strictly oppose "ethics dumping," a practice where experimental or high-risk AI systems are deployed in Global South nations lacking robust regulatory safeguards, effectively treating marginalized populations as beta testers [cite: 21, 22]. Furthermore, they demand absolute prohibitions on specific harmful use cases, including real-time biometric surveillance in public spaces, social credit scoring systems, and AI-driven autonomous weapons that operate without meaningful human control [cite: 11, 12, 18]. Another critical red line is regulatory imperialism; they reject the uncritical imposition of Global North regulatory frameworks, which act as prohibitive compliance burdens that criminalize local innovation and reinforce foreign monopolies [cite: 4]. Additionally, they categorically refuse any governance model that mandates the free extraction and cross-border flow of data out of the Global South without reciprocating protections for digital knowledge sovereignty and fair economic return [cite: 23].

### Strategic Tradeoffs
While defending these boundaries, Global South civil society leaders are pragmatic and willing to negotiate strategic concessions to secure infrastructural capacity. For instance, while they fiercely protect data sovereignty, they are willing to concede strict national data localization rules if such mandates hinder cross-border collaboration within the Global South. In exchange for establishing shared, regional public compute infrastructures that bypass foreign cloud monopolies, they will accept regionalized data sharing [cite: 24, 25]. Furthermore, they demonstrate a willingness to operate within established international intellectual property and trade frameworks—despite deep critiques of their exclusionary nature—provided these regimes explicitly incorporate binding technology transfer mandates, capacity-building programs, and climate-finance-style funding mechanisms aimed at closing the global AI divide [cite: 8, 26, 27].

| Stakeholder Group | Relationship Dynamics | Points of Alignment / Contention |

| **UN Agencies & Multilateral Bodies** (e.g., OHCHR, UNDP, ITU) | **Primary Allies** | Alignment on anchoring AI governance in international human rights law, sustainable development goals (SDGs), and inclusive multilateralism [cite: 28, 29]. |
| **Global South Middle Powers** (e.g., Brazil, South Africa, India) | **Strategic Partners** | Shared focus on digital sovereignty and Digital Public Infrastructure (DPI). Occasional friction arises when state security priorities override civil liberties and domestic data privacy [cite: 13, 30]. |

## Multistakeholder Engagement Style
The public record reveals a highly sophisticated, dual-track engagement strategy employed by these leaders. In formal statements and plenaries—such as negotiations for the Global Digital Compact or WSIS+20—their style is normative, rights-based, and highly critical of power asymmetries. They utilize these platforms to highlight structural inequalities, condemn "ethics dumping," and frame AI governance as an imperative of decolonization and international justice [cite: 21, 22, 33]. They consistently demand that the UN and other bodies ensure civil society is granted actual agenda-setting power, explicitly criticizing processes that offer superficial "visibility" without granting structural influence over final outcome documents [cite: 34, 35].

In working sessions, technical committees, and advisory bodies, their engagement shifts to an evidence-based, infrastructural focus. They leverage localized data gathered through Global South "AI observatories" to propose highly specific, implementable mechanisms that challenge technosolutionist narratives [cite: 36]. Within these sessions, they translate broad justice claims into actionable policy tools, focusing heavily on data governance standards, public procurement guidelines, bias audit methodologies, and architectural frameworks for public compute allocation [cite: 1, 8, 37]. This dual approach allows them to operate effectively as both moral challengers to the status quo and technical architects of a more equitable digital future.
