

## 3. Red Lines: Non-Negotiable Thresholds
Academic safety researchers have formalized several strict "red lines"—behaviors or capabilities that must be universally prohibited under international law, often highlighting these in prominent consensus frameworks such as the International Dialogues on AI Safety (IDAIS) Beijing and Shanghai statements [cite: 28, 29, 30]. 

| Risk Category | Prohibited Behavior / Capability Threshold |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Autonomous Replication** | Systems must not copy themselves or recursively enhance their own code/capabilities without explicit human approval [cite: 31, 32]. |
| **CBRN Weapons Assistance** | Systems must not substantially increase an actor's ability to design chemical, biological, radiological, or nuclear weapons [cite: 31, 33]. |
| **Cyber-Offense** | Systems must not autonomously execute cyberattacks resulting in serious financial loss, physical harm, or critical infrastructure disruption [cite: 31, 32]. |
| **Power Seeking & Deception** | AI must not take actions to unduly increase its influence, nor consistently deceive regulators regarding its likelihood of crossing safety thresholds [cite: 31, 32]. |
| **Lethal Autonomy** | The delegation of life-taking decisions to machines is fundamentally rejected; humans must remain in the loop for kinetic military action [cite: 1, 34]. |
| **Shutdown Resistance** | Systems must possess a legally mandated "termination obligation," ensuring they can be reliably deactivated without resistance [cite: 1, 30]. |

## 4. Tradeoffs: The "Alignment Tax"
To guarantee safety, this stakeholder is entirely willing to concede raw system performance, development speed, and deployment efficiency—a compromise known within the field as the "alignment tax" [cite: 13, 14, 35]. Researchers acknowledge that safety fine-tuning often degrades a model's general capabilities, particularly in dual-use domains where legitimate and harmful capabilities are closely entangled [cite: 13]. They are willing to accept this tax, arguing that a highly capable but misaligned system is fundamentally useless and dangerous. Furthermore, they are willing to accept limitations on academic freedom (e.g., restricting open-source distributions of frontier models) and tolerate extensive bureaucratic friction, including data minimization practices that may conflict with capability scaling, in exchange for security guarantees and verifiable alignment [cite: 9, 36, 37].

## 5. Typical Allies and Points of Friction
This stakeholder frequently partners with civil society organizations, human rights advocates, national AI Safety Institutes, and risk-aware policymakers who prioritize long-term global stability over short-term economic gains [cite: 26, 38, 39]. These alliances center on establishing verifiable safety protocols and managing structural risks.

Conversely, deep friction exists with frontier lab executives and investors who prioritize rapid deployment, market dominance, and commercialization, often treating safety as a secondary concern or marketing tool [cite: 4, 13, 22, 25]. Strong ideological clashes also occur with open-source proponents who view AI as a democratizing force and fiercely oppose computing thresholds and weight restrictions as monopolistic regulatory capture [cite: 9, 10]. Additionally, researchers routinely battle narratives from techno-accelerationists who view safety concerns as unwarranted "doomerism" that unfairly stifles innovation and geopolitical competitiveness [cite: 21, 40, 41].

## 6. Engagement in Multistakeholder Meetings
Academic safety researchers utilize a sophisticated dual-track engagement strategy, balancing highly visible public advocacy with deep technocratic integration [cite: 42, 43]. In the public sphere, they frequently utilize highly publicized, multi-signatory open letters and consensus statements—such as the Future of Life Institute's 6-month pause letter, the Center for AI Safety's extinction statement, and the IDAIS red lines declarations—to force political consensus and clearly delineate moral boundaries [cite: 4, 28, 44, 45]. 

However, in closed-door or multistakeholder summits, such as the Bletchley Park AI Safety Summit, UN Security Council briefings, and the AI Action Summit, these experts act as technocratic architects. They eschew emotional appeals in favor of producing rigorous, empirical documents—such as the *International Scientific Report on the Safety of Advanced AI*—to establish scientific baselines, propose specific risk-management frameworks, and design complex auditing architectures [cite: 4, 26, 39, 46, 47].
