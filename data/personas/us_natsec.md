

## Allies and Points of Friction

The U.S. approach to AI governance inherently creates a complex web of alliances and geopolitical friction points, dictated by the advisor's mandate to secure technological supremacy.

| Stakeholder Group | Relationship Dynamic | Core Driver of Alignment or Friction |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Domestic Frontier AI Labs & Defense Contractors** | **Primary Allies** | Shared interest in maintaining U.S. technological leadership, securing massive energy/infrastructure investments, and advancing capabilities through flexible, pro-innovation regulatory environments [cite: 6, 27]. |
| **G7 Nations & Five Eyes Intelligence Alliance** | **Strategic Allies** | Alignment on plurilateral export controls, intelligence sharing, and the establishment of unified procurement standards to secure the global hardware supply chain against adversaries [cite: 24, 34, 47]. |
| **European Union Regulators** | **Point of Friction** | The EU prioritizes a precautionary, rights-based approach to AI regulation, which U.S. national security apparatus views as a hindrance to technological competitiveness and rapid military adoption [cite: 17, 48, 49]. |
| **Safety-First Corporate Actors** | **Point of Friction** | Tension frequently flares with AI developers attempting to impose strict ethical or anti-military contractual restrictions on the Department of Defense, resulting in highly publicized procurement disputes [cite: 18, 50, 51]. |
| **Strategic Competitors (e.g., China)** | **Primary Adversaries** | Systemic friction driven by U.S. semiconductor export controls, efforts to dominate the global digital infrastructure, and mutual accusations of AI-enabled cyber espionage [cite: 31, 52, 53]. |

## Multistakeholder Engagement Strategy

In multistakeholder environments, the national security advisor eschews performative, highly publicized treaty negotiations in favor of closed-door, targeted working sessions [cite: 54, 55, 56]. Public engagement relies heavily on formal policy declarations and memorandums (such as the National Security Memorandum on AI) to signal strength, establish doctrine, and dictate federal procurement behavior [cite: 5, 8]. 

Privately, the advisor utilizes Track II dialogues and discrete bilateral channels to negotiate practical, highly specific crisis-management tools. A primary example of this is the pursuit of a U.S.-China AI incident hotline. This mechanism is aimed specifically at preventing automated escalation in cyber or nuclear domains through rapid communication, allowing both superpowers to manage existential risks without requiring either side to commit to broader, restrictive arms-control treaties that might compromise their respective technological advantages [cite: 25, 44, 47, 57]. This dual-track approach ensures that international engagement remains strictly focused on actionable national security outcomes rather than becoming constrained by international bureaucracy.
