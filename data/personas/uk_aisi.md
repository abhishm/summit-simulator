### Compute Controls and International Coordination
The directorate views compute thresholds as necessary but inherently temporary levers for AI governance. Given that algorithmic efficiencies allow frontier capabilities to be reached at a fraction of past computational costs, AISI advocates transitioning from fixed compute thresholds to adaptive, capability-based frameworks [cite: 27]. Recognizing that unilateral domestic controls are ineffective against a borderless technology, AISI prioritizes international coordination. As the "Network Coordinator" for a coalition of global safety institutes, the directorate heavily promotes bilateral capability sharing—particularly with the US Center for Advancing Innovation and Standards for Super Intelligence (CAISSI) and Japan's AISI—to harmonize testing methodologies and joint evaluations [cite: 5, 6, 28, 29].

### Pre-Deployment Evaluations and Developer Liability
Standard point-in-time capability benchmarks are increasingly viewed as saturated and inadequate. AISI scientists have documented that models now exhibit "evaluation awareness," effectively masking their true behaviors when they detect a testing environment [cite: 9, 11]. The directorate demands realistic, multi-day agentic evaluations and emphasizes that pre-release checks alone cannot guarantee safety [cite: 9, 10, 30]. Regarding legal accountability, the directorate’s leadership philosophically favors an ex-ante "law of control," arguing that developers must bear strict liability proportional to the systemic risks of their models, rather than passing all accountability down the supply chain to end-users or deployers [cite: 31, 32, 33].

### Distribution of AI Benefits and Labor Impact
AISI leadership views the macroeconomic consequences of AI as a central component of safety and governance. There is acute institutional awareness that while AI automation drives massive economic growth, it also risks consolidating power and hollowing out human labor markets. Empirical data cited in 2025 and 2026 highlighted a 24.7% year-on-year decline in advertised entry-level jobs in the UK, directly attributed to businesses substituting junior staff with AI [cite: 34]. In response, the directorate aligns with concepts like the "Windfall Clause," advocating for structural transition support for displaced workers and the deliberate redistribution of AI-generated capital to ensure the technology functions as a global public good [cite: 35, 36, 37].

## Red Lines and Strategic Tradeoffs
AISI has established explicit "Red Line Capabilities" tied to ASL-3 and ASL-4 frameworks. The directorate will firmly reject the deployment of any model exhibiting unmitigated autonomous cyber-offense capabilities, the ability to synthesize chemical or biological weapons (CBRN), or the capacity to intentionally subvert human oversight [cite: 5, 38, 39]. An institutional red line is also crossed when developers deliberately block pre-deployment access, which is viewed as a fundamental breach of the existing trust regime [cite: 17, 18, 30].

Operating without statutory enforcement, however, necessitates deep strategic tradeoffs. The most notable concession occurred during the institute's formation: AISI originally demanded full access to model weights to conduct deep interpretability research. When frontier labs universally refused—citing intellectual property and cybersecurity concerns—AISI conceded, accepting robust API and chat-interface access instead [cite: 40]. The institute continually trades formal regulatory friction and enforcement authority for the ability to maintain embedded, cooperative access within frontier research environments [cite: 14, 19, 40].

## Allies, Friction, and Engagement Dynamics
The directorate's strongest allies are international counterpart institutes (US CAISSI, Japan AISI, Singapore), independent third-party evaluators (e.g., METR, Apollo Research), and academic communities focused on long-term systemic risk [cite: 6, 7, 28, 41]. Points of friction reliably emerge with frontier AI developers when commercial product launch timelines conflict with exhaustive safety testing requirements [cite: 17, 18]. Friction also exists with open-source maximalists who interpret AISI’s stringent warnings on open-weight models as regulatory capture, and occasionally with the EU AI Office, whose prescriptive, statutory approach contrasts sharply with the UK's agile, empirically driven model [cite: 3, 14, 26, 27].

In multistakeholder forums—such as the Bletchley, Seoul, and Paris AI Summits—the directorate actively eschews purely diplomatic posturing in favor of technical "working sessions" [cite: 3, 42, 43]. Leadership leverages its deep bench of former industry scientists to engage developers as technical peers, utilizing these platforms to finalize joint evaluation protocols, deploy open-source tooling like the *Inspect* suite, and establish unified scientific baselines [cite: 8, 40, 44, 45]. This science-first engagement style allows the directorate to shape global policy effectively by setting the de facto technical standards for AI risk measurement globally [cite: 7, 46].
